Three questions

Why is my sand not the Hickory Sands District?

Sandy ground is not enough on its own: Notice No. 243 notes “hickory sands” soils in the proposed Llano Uplift AVA as well.1, 11 TTB proposes that only the full name be protected, so that wine labels can still truthfully mention “hickory sand” soils.1 The district is drawn around the sandstone at 1,500 feet and higher;1 what the petition relies on is soil depth over the sandstone, together with its groundwater.2, 11

Why does my well find granite?

The Hickory Sandstone Member lies on much older granite, gneiss and schist.2, 5 A well through the sandstone can reach that rock at the bottom of the hole.7 Where the old rock is at the surface, the sandstone is absent,5, 10 and most wells reach granite or other old rock beneath a thin cover.7

Where is the aquifer deep, and where at the surface?

At the surface where the sandstone crops out, in and around the district; rain falling there recharges it.5, 9 Away from the core it dips under younger rock:5 at the slice’s north-west end its top is about 2,240 feet down in TWDB’s model.8 Under the core itself it is absent.5, 10

Deep time

How the sand
came to be.

Eight stages, from the oldest rock to the ground today. The small numbers lead to the sources.

  1. 01

    More than a billion years ago

    Approximate: ranges from published summaries of zircon dating, not dates for single rocks.

    The basement

    Sediments and volcanic rock gathered along the southern edge of ancestral North America and in a volcanic arc offshore, from about 1,370 million years ago.12, 13 A collision of continents, about 1,150 to 1,120 million years ago, buried them deep and folded them into the Valley Spring Gneiss and the Packsaddle Schist; the folds show in road cuts on US 87 south of Mason.14, 15, 16 Granites rose into them as the collision ended, the Town Mountain Granite among them, about 1,120 to 1,070 million years ago.12, 13

  2. 02

    About 1,070 million to roughly half a billion years ago

    The gap is certain; its length varies: more than 500 million years where the sand rests on granite, more where it rests on the older gneiss. The dates mark the gap in the rock, not a measured span of erosion.

    The missing time

    By the Cambrian the old rock was a rolling surface of granite knobs and hollows, with up to about 800 feet of relief by one estimate, and the granite had crumbled in place to grus.5, 16, 17 Where sand later buried that surface, rock more than a billion years old lies directly under rock about half a billion years old: the Great Unconformity.16, 18 This stage shows that surface as it stood just before the sand came, not every landscape before it.

    The unconformity marks missing time, not a measured span of erosion: the rock that might have recorded those hundreds of millions of years is not here.16 Dating of zircons from the uplift’s rock, as one review summarizes it, records more than 6 kilometers of rock worn off above it between about 800 and 750 million years ago, likely as the flank of a rift was raised while the supercontinent Rodinia broke apart.12 How the wider unconformity formed is still argued: one prominent idea is glacial erosion in the Snowball Earth episode; others see many smaller unconformities from Rodinia’s assembly and breakup.12

  3. 03

    Roughly half a billion years ago

    Dated by its fossils, not by radioactive decay, so the age is approximate; the lowest beds hold no fossils and may be somewhat older in places.

    The Cambrian sea

    A shallow sea spread across North America and drowned the hills.5, 12 This was a marine transgression: the shoreline moved inland across the land as the sea spread.16, 18 On its beaches and tidal flats, in its estuaries and tidal channels, sand that rivers, fans and wind had spread over the old surface was worked and reworked into the Hickory Sandstone Member of the Riley Formation: thick in the hollows, thin or absent over the knobs.5, 16, 17, 18, 19 Trilobites in it, some collected at Streeter in western Mason County, date it.20, 21, 22, 23

    Near the old granite hills, its lowest beds hold pebbles and cobbles of the local granite; at one road cut on Farm Road 2323, cross-bedded sand reworked from the granite’s grus lies directly on the weathered granite.4, 16 Rivers and wind also brought sand from far inland to the coast, where it too was reworked.18 Of more than 1,700 zircon grains dated from the Hickory Sandstone Member around the uplift, those from its western side, where the district lies, came mostly from older basement far to the north and north-west (as directions run on today’s map), itself largely granite and rhyolite; those from the eastern side came mostly from the Llano’s own rock.18 Zircon is a tracer: the counts are of grains, not shares of the sand.

  4. 04

    About 500 to 470 million years ago

    The order of the layers is certain. The numbers are approximate: fossil zones matched to the international time scale.23

    Under later seas

    The sea deepened. Limestone and more sand, the Cap Mountain Limestone and Lion Mountain Sandstone members, completed the Riley Formation. The Wilberns Formation followed, laid down on a shallow shelf; its microbial mounds still show on the Llano River at White’s Crossing, south of Mason. Then came the limestone and dolomite of the Ellenburger Group.5, 16, 20, 24 In this first burial, which lasted until the end of the Early Ordovician, more new minerals grew between the sand grains, calcite among them.19

  5. 05

    Roughly 323 to 307 million years ago

    The sources say early to middle Pennsylvanian; the numbers are that span on the international time scale, not a measured date.23

    The dome

    After the Ellenburger Group was laid down, the area was raised and worn down more than once, and later seas left limestone and shale, much of it since worn away; the drawing leaves these layers out.5 When the southern continents collided with North America and raised the Ouachita mountain belt, the Llano area was pushed up into a dome and broken along north-east-trending faults, with throws of up to about 3,000 feet, that dropped younger rock against older.5, 16, 19 The Hickory Sandstone Member was tilted and faulted with the rest.5, 19 Its deepest burial is thought to have come about this time, possibly in the middle Pennsylvanian. Where it lay deeper than 3 kilometers, quartz cement filled most of the pores between its grains; where it was never buried more than 1 kilometer, it has stayed porous and permeable.19

  6. 06

    About 299 to 100 million years ago

    Not dated closely: the long wearing-down from the Permian to the Jurassic, and when the sea first reached the area and finally covered the uplift, both in the Early Cretaceous.23

    The Cretaceous sea

    Through the Permian, Triassic and Jurassic the region was raised and worn down to a low, rolling surface cut across the tilted and faulted layers, probably bringing the Hickory Sandstone Member near the surface again.5, 19, 25 Then a sea returned from the south-east. The Llano high stood for a time as a promontory, then was largely buried under the sea’s sand and limestone; at Bear Mountain, near Fredericksburg, Cretaceous rock rests directly on granite.5, 16, 25 So the Cretaceous rests on whichever older rock that surface had reached: Paleozoic layers in some places, and in others the Precambrian rock itself, where the layers above had been worn away or never laid down. The contact is a second unconformity, at the base of the Cretaceous, far younger than the Great Unconformity.5, 16, 25

  7. 07

    Within the last 66 million years

    Not dated closely. Published work places a later uplift of the region in the Neogene, about 23 to 2.6 million years ago, and the Balcones faults south-east of the uplift mostly in the late Oligocene and early Miocene; we found no study that dates when the cover came off any one place, the district among them, so the stage is shown as one long interval.19, 23, 25, 26

    Uncovered

    In the Neogene the region rose again, as part of the wider uplift of the Cordillera.26 South-east of the uplift the Balcones faults broke the Cretaceous rock, and new streams cut back from them toward the plateau; the streams are still cutting down into the uplifted rock.25 The rise, the stripping of each layer of cover and the cutting of the valleys need not have happened at the same time.

    Erosion stripped the Cretaceous cover from the center of the old dome and cut down into it, as a knife takes the top off an onion: the oldest rock, granite and gneiss, at the core; the Hickory Sandstone Member in a ring around it, dipping away in every direction; younger rock beyond.5, 17, 19 Over the core the sandstone is gone, down to the old rock beneath; TWDB’s modelers judge that the sandstone’s thinning toward the uplift’s center probably owes more to erosion than to how the sand was first laid down.5 The old rock wears down faster than the rim of younger, harder rock around it.5 The Llano Uplift today is both: a basin with a rolling floor, and rounded hills of Precambrian granite rising 400 to 600 feet within it, such as the Enchanted Rock granite dome.5

    As the region rose in the Neogene, water from rain soaked into the sandstone, dissolving feldspar, about 5 percent by one estimate, and an unknown amount of calcite. Some beds, once rich in feldspar, had lost most or all of it over their long history and are now nearly pure quartz sandstone. Cement closed pores; dissolution opened new ones. Where the sandstone was never deeply buried, its original pores and the secondary pores opened by dissolution together make an excellent aquifer.19

  8. 08

    Today

    The soils are not dated.

    Sand and water

    Weathering is turning the Hickory Sandstone Member back into loose sand. Rain falling on the outcrop recharges the Hickory Aquifer. Where the sandstone dips away under younger rock its water is confined, under pressure; the aquifer supplies Brady, Mason and Fredericksburg.5, 6, 9 The sandstone’s age is not its water’s: carbon-14 and tritium find younger water near the outcrop in Gillespie, Mason and McCulloch counties, where rain recharges it, and older water downdip in McCulloch County.5

Water under the ground

Three wells,
three rocks.

In and around the proposed Hickory Sands District, wells a few miles apart differ widely in depth, water level and yield, by the rock they are drilled in.7 Here are three typical wells, grouped by mapped geology and inferred aquifer: one on the Hickory Sandstone Member of the Riley Formation, one on Precambrian granite, and one far downdip, where the member lies deep under younger rock.9, 27, 4

  1. 01

    The three places

    On the outcrop, the Hickory Sandstone Member is at the surface.4 On the granite, Precambrian granite is at the surface, Town Mountain Granite and the younger granitic intrusions; the member has “zero thickness at the Precambrian granite knobs.”10, 4

    Away from the uplift the member dips under younger rock, away from the uplift in all directions; in McCulloch County “northward and northwestward at about 120 feet per mile.”9, 27 The downdip well drawn here stands for wells where TWDB’s model puts the member’s top 1,500 to 3,000 feet down.8, 7

    Outside the member’s surface outcrop, wells may still reach the Hickory Sandstone Member where it continues underground beneath younger rocks.27, 28 This differs from areas where Precambrian granite or gneiss is at the surface: that older basement lies beneath the member in the normal geological sequence.10, 11 The granite examples shown here do not demonstrate a connection to the Hickory Aquifer.

  2. 02

    Where the water is, and how it reaches the well

    Outcrop. The water is held “in the spaces between the sand grains.” “Water occurs under water-table (unconfined) conditions in much of the outcrop area … and under artesian (confined) conditions in the rest of the county.” The lower part of the member is coarser and more productive, and “is tapped by most of the larger capacity wells in the county.”9 Water moves through the connected pore spaces of the saturated sandstone into the well’s producing interval, a screen or open hole; the drawing’s completion is schematic.

    Granite. Water “occurs in fractures and in the weathered zone in the outcrop area,” yielding “small quantities … to domestic and stock wells.”9 USGS puts it plainly: “Spaces in crystalline rocks are microscopically small, few, and generally unconnected,” and “movement of water through the rocks is totally dependent on the presence of secondary openings”; these rocks “are permeable only where they are fractured.”29 Two kinds of granite aquifer are described in Burnet County. Weathered granite gravel, the “Granite Gravel Aquifer,” is “composed of weathered or decomposed Town Mountain Granite,” saturated from a few feet to about 100 feet, with estimated yields “from as little as 5 gpm up to 100+ gpm.”30 Fractured solid granite is “generally suitable only for domestic use because well yields are typically low (less than 25 gpm) and many cannot sustain continuous pumping.”30 These describe Burnet County, where yields vary widely; they are not measured yields for wells elsewhere. In TWDB’s model the granite stores far less: storativity 0.001, against 0.09–0.3 on the member’s outcrop.8

    The example granite well. The main granite drawing is one documented well, TWDB driller’s report 524179 (Llano County, 2019), drawn to its own depth scale; it is not the granite group’s median. The report records topsoil to 1 foot, red granite gravel to 21 feet and gray granite to 145 feet; blank PVC casing to 21 feet, with open hole below; water at 124–127 feet; a static level of 31 feet, measured by steel tape on October 7, 2019; and a jetted test of 12.24 gpm.31 The well receives water through connected openings within granite. The report does not establish where the connected fracture network is recharged. It identifies the rock and the water-bearing depth; it does not map fractures, so the fractures are drawn schematically, as inferred flow through fractures, with intact granite between them.29, 31 Blank casing across the shallow gravel prevents direct entry through the casing, but does not prove the surrounding fracture network is disconnected from weathered material elsewhere.

    Screened wells in gravel and granite. Where granite gravel and weathered granite lie over the granite, a well may instead be screened through the loose material and into the rock below: in loose material “a screen is ‘telescoped’ inside the casing.”32 The other granite view draws such a well, with rounded illustrative depths and values. Water can enter through connected spaces in the gravel and weathered material, and through water-bearing fractures intersecting the screen.33, 29 The sources do not establish each pathway’s contribution, so the flow paths are schematic.

    In solid rock, “the normal practice is to ‘seat’ the casing firmly in the top of the rock and drill an open hole to the depth required to obtain the needed yield.”32 The example granite well is built that way; the outcrop and downdip completions are schematic.

    Downdip. Under younger rock the member’s water is confined, under artesian pressure.9 Water moves through the confined sandstone into the well’s producing interval, perforated casing or a screen in the drawing (schematic), and rises inside the well (step 04).

  3. 03

    Where it comes from

    Outcrop. Recharge is “chiefly from precipitation on the outcrop.”9 “Most recharge to the Hickory … is from rainfall on the sandy outcrop, but a significant amount occurs where rivers and tributaries cross the outcrops.”27 The drawing’s rain arrows are conceptual: they show that rain on the outcrop recharges the sandstone, not the route to any one well.

    Granite. Recharge and flow are predominantly local: rain soaks into the weathered layer, and the aquifer has “low storage and shallow localized flow systems.”34 TWDB’s model puts average recharge on the Precambrian outcrop at 0.18 inches a year, against 1.09 on the member’s outcrop.8 For the example well the recharge route is not documented, so its recharge arrows are conceptual.31

    Downdip. The water enters on the outcrop and moves slowly downdip, as “underflow.”9 The faint dashed arrow shows a possible long-term groundwater pathway (conceptual); it does not mean recent rain quickly replenishes deep wells. Carbon-14 and tritium find younger and mixed water near the recharge zone and slightly downdip in Gillespie, Mason and McCulloch counties, and older groundwater downdip in McCulloch County: “The downdip wells in McCulloch County have very low radiocarbon activities …, suggesting long groundwater residence time.”5

    • Conceptual pathway, not documented

  4. 04

    Water levels

    Each well shows its static water level, where the water stands at rest. In an outcrop well the median static level is 61 feet, 68 in irrigation wells; in the granite group, 30 feet.7 The outcrop well’s pumping level is drawn with an assumed drawdown of 20 to 60 feet: only 3 outcrop tests record one.7 The granite example’s static level is 31 feet; no pumping level was measured, so none is drawn.31 In the screened gravel-and-granite drawing the static level, about 30 feet, is illustrative, and no pumping level is drawn. Its jetted test of about 20 gpm is illustrative too: not a measured yield for any location, and neither it nor any single test establishes a Hickory Aquifer completion or sustained irrigation capacity.

    Downdip, the aquifer and the water level are far apart. The member’s top is about 2,150 feet down at the median in TWDB’s model; the water rises inside the well to a static level of about 306 feet (the median of the latest TWDB measurements, 34 wells) to about 416 feet (the median at completion on drillers’ reports, 34 wells).8, 7 Confined water “will rise in wells above the level at which it is first encountered”: it is the water in the well that rises, not the aquifer.9 Pumping draws it down about 50 feet more: the median drawdown on 25 recorded tests, some estimated or jetted.7 Levels are “more than 400 feet below the land surface in some Hickory … wells far downdip.”27

  6. 06

    What is not known

    Does a granite well ever draw Hickory Aquifer water through fractures? The sources reviewed do not establish Hickory Aquifer water supplying these granite wells, and the drawing does not show it. Driller’s reports record where water was reported, and reported water encounters do not necessarily identify which zones supply the finished well; they do not establish the water’s age, rainfall dependence, or connection to the Hickory Aquifer.7 Mason describes flow the other way near the outcrop: water “moves through cracks in the Precambrian rocks into the Hickory.”9 TWDB’s conceptual model assumes no exchange with the Precambrian “due to the low permeability of the unweathered portions.”5 Deeper fractures may allow a “potential hydrologic connection to a deeper regional flow regime,” without naming the Hickory Aquifer.34

    Drought. This comparison does not show how any well responds to drought. That granite wells are sensitive to drought rests on a conference abstract and a general USGS model: a stated risk, not a measured rate.34, 33 Nor are sandstone wells shown as drought-proof: in outcrop wells far from pumping, water levels “are high in the spring when the precipitation is greatest and low in the summer,” and “Fluctuations of considerable magnitude are caused by pumping and are especially conspicuous in the artesian part of the aquifer.”9

How these numbers were made

Every figure in this section is counted by the site’s build from the sources below (src/site/make_wells.py); the drawings are drawn from the same figures.

  • Data: TWDB Submitted Driller’s Reports and Groundwater Database, bulk downloads of October 16, 2026, read from the site’s privacy-scrubbed well records (38,867 records).7
  • Counties: Gillespie, Kimble, Llano, Mason, McCulloch, Menard and San Saba, by the 2023 Census county boundaries.
  • Groups (by mapped geology and inferred aquifer): the mapped rock at the well, on USGS’s regional geologic map as the site shifts it, and, downdip, the depth to the member in TWDB’s model.4, 8 This does not establish each well’s producing formation; completion records were not used to confirm each assignment. As a check, where drillers logged the water-bearing stratum, 179 of 236 logs in the outcrop group (76%) name sandstone and 37 (16%) granite, and 326 of 377 in the granite group (86%) name granite; the deep group has only 4 such logs. Outcrop: the Hickory Sandstone Member at the surface. Granite: Town Mountain Granite or a younger granite at the surface. Downdip: younger rock at the surface, and the well either coded Hickory Aquifer by TWDB or drilled to or below the top of the member in TWDB’s groundwater model. The deep downdip well: downdip wells where the model puts that top 1,500 to 3,000 feet down.
  • Depth: the completed depth; a depth recorded as zero is taken as not known. Yield: the largest test on each report, with 0-gpm tests kept. Water level: TWDB’s latest measurement, or the driller’s level at completion. Drawdown: of the tests on each record that give both a yield and a drawdown, the one with the highest specific capacity.
  • The granite example: TWDB driller’s report 524179, read from the site’s published well records. Its lithology, casing, borehole, water-bearing interval, static level and test are drawn as reported; its fractures are schematic.31
  • Moving water and arrows: every arrow that shows water entering a well ends on a screen or an open hole, never on blank casing; the build checks this from the drawing itself. Conceptual pathways are drawn faint and dashed and labelled. With reduced motion the water does not move and the arrows stay.
  • Lift energy: 1.55 kWh per acre-foot per foot of lift, with lift = static level + drawdown.35
  • Costs: read between the rows and columns of the Uniform Costing Model tables (memo Table 14, public supply, p. 30; Table 16, irrigation, p. 32; January 2023 dollars), raised to July 2026 (× 1.0236 to the 2026 Region F plan’s September 2023 level, then × 1.0609 by the ENR index). The irrigation table stops at 2,000 ft and is not extended.36, 37, 38
