Notice No. 243 describes the line as drawn “to encompass the portions of the Hickory Sandstone Member geologic formation that are at 1,500 feet or higher.”1 The petition names the key elements that ground imparts: “the Hickory aquifer, Hickory Sands soils, and elevation at 1,500 feet or higher.”2

Three questions

Why is my sand not the Hickory Sands District?

Sandy ground is not enough on its own: Notice No. 243 notes “hickory sands” soils in the proposed Llano Uplift AVA as well.1, 11 TTB proposes that only the full name be protected, so that wine labels can still truthfully mention “hickory sand” soils.1 The district is drawn around the formation at 1,500 feet and higher;1 what the petition relies on is soil depth over the formation, together with the formation’s groundwater.2, 11

Why does my well find granite?

The Hickory Sandstone Member formation lies on much older granite, gneiss and schist.2, 5 A well through the sandstone can reach that rock at the bottom of the hole.7 Where the old rock is at the surface, the formation is absent,5, 10 and most wells reach granite or other old rock beneath a thin cover.7

Where is the aquifer deep, and where at the surface?

At the surface where the formation crops out, in and around the district; rain falling there recharges it.5, 9 Away from the core it dips under younger rock:5 at the slice’s north-west end its top is about 2,240 feet down in TWDB’s model.8 Under the core itself it is absent.5, 10

Deep time

How the sand
came to be.

Eight stages, from the oldest rock to the ground today. The small numbers lead to the sources.

  1. 01

    More than a billion years ago

    Approximate: ranges from published summaries of zircon dating, not dates for single rocks.

    The basement

    Sediments and volcanic rock gathered along the southern edge of ancestral North America and in a volcanic arc offshore, from about 1,370 million years ago.12, 13 A collision of continents, about 1,150 to 1,120 million years ago, buried them deep and folded them into the Valley Spring Gneiss and the Packsaddle Schist; the folds show in road cuts on US 87 south of Mason.14, 15, 16 Granites rose into them as the collision ended, the Town Mountain Granite among them, about 1,120 to 1,070 million years ago.12, 13

  2. 02

    About 1,070 million to roughly half a billion years ago

    The gap is certain; its length varies: more than 500 million years where the sand rests on granite, more where it rests on the older gneiss.

    The long erosion

    For more than 500 million years the mountains wore away. By the Cambrian the old rock was a rolling surface of granite knobs and hollows, with up to about 800 feet of relief by one estimate, and the granite had crumbled in place to grus.5, 16, 17 Where sand later buried that surface, rock more than a billion years old lies directly under rock about half a billion years old: the Great Unconformity.16, 18

  3. 03

    Roughly half a billion years ago

    Dated by its fossils, not by radioactive decay, so the age is approximate; the lowest beds hold no fossils and may be somewhat older in places.

    The Cambrian sea

    A shallow sea spread across North America and drowned the hills.5, 12 On its beaches and tidal flats, in its estuaries and tidal channels, sand that rivers, fans and wind had spread over the old surface was worked and reworked into the Hickory Sandstone Member of the Riley Formation: thick in the hollows, thin or absent over the knobs.5, 16, 17, 18, 19 Trilobites in it, some collected at Streeter in western Mason County, date it.20, 21, 22, 23

    Near the old granite hills, its lowest beds hold pebbles and cobbles of the local granite.4, 16 Rivers and wind also brought sand from far inland. Of more than 1,700 zircon grains dated from the Hickory Sandstone Member around the uplift, those from its western side, where the district lies, came mostly from older basement far to the north and north-west, itself largely granite and rhyolite; those from the eastern side came mostly from the Llano’s own rock.18 Zircon is a tracer: the counts are of grains, not shares of the sand.

  4. 04

    About 500 to 470 million years ago

    The order of the layers is certain. The numbers are approximate: fossil zones matched to the international time scale.23

    Under later seas

    The sea deepened. Limestone and more sand, the Cap Mountain Limestone and Lion Mountain Sandstone members, completed the Riley Formation. The Wilberns Formation followed, laid down on a shallow shelf; its microbial mounds still show on the Llano River at White’s Crossing, south of Mason. Then came the limestone and dolomite of the Ellenburger Group.5, 16, 20, 24 In this first burial, which lasted until the end of the Early Ordovician, new minerals began to grow between the sand grains, calcite among them.19

  5. 05

    Roughly 323 to 307 million years ago

    The sources say early to middle Pennsylvanian; the numbers are that span on the international time scale, not a measured date.23

    The dome

    When the southern continents collided with North America and raised the Ouachita mountain belt, the Llano area was pushed up into a dome and broken along north-east-trending faults, with throws of up to about 3,000 feet, that dropped younger rock against older.5, 16, 19 The Hickory Sandstone Member was tilted and faulted with the rest.5, 19 Its deepest burial is thought to have come about this time, possibly in the middle Pennsylvanian. Where it lay deeper than 3 kilometers, quartz cement filled most of the pores between its grains; where it was never buried more than 1 kilometer, it has stayed porous and permeable.19

  6. 06

    About 299 to 100 million years ago

    Not dated closely: the long wearing-down from the Permian to the Jurassic, and when the sea first reached the area and finally covered the uplift, both in the Early Cretaceous.23

    The Cretaceous sea

    Through the Permian, Triassic and Jurassic the region was raised and worn down, probably bringing the Hickory Sandstone Member near the surface again.19, 25 Then a sea returned from the south-east. The Llano high stood for a time as a promontory, then was largely buried under the sea’s sand and limestone; at Bear Mountain, near Fredericksburg, Cretaceous rock rests directly on granite.5, 16, 25

  7. 07

    Within the last 66 million years

    Not dated: we found no study of when the cover was stripped, so it is shown as one long interval.

    Uncovered

    The region rose again, and erosion stripped the Cretaceous cover from the center of the old dome and cut down into it, as a knife takes the top off an onion: the oldest rock, granite and gneiss, at the core; the Hickory Sandstone Member in a ring around it, dipping away in every direction; younger rock beyond.5, 17, 19 The old rock wears down faster than the rim of younger, harder rock around it.5 The Llano Uplift today is both: a basin with a rolling floor, and rounded hills of Precambrian granite rising 400 to 600 feet within it, such as the Enchanted Rock granite dome.5

    As the region rose in the Neogene, water from rain soaked into the sandstone, dissolving feldspar, about 5 percent by one estimate, and an unknown amount of calcite. Some beds, once rich in feldspar, had lost most or all of it over their long history and are now nearly pure quartz sandstone. Cement closed pores; dissolution opened new ones. Where the sandstone was never deeply buried, its original pores and these later, secondary pores together make an excellent aquifer.19

  8. 08

    Today

    The soils are not dated.

    Sand and water

    Weathering is turning the Hickory Sandstone Member back into loose sand. Rain falling on the outcrop recharges the Hickory Aquifer. Where the sandstone dips away under younger rock its water is confined, under pressure; the aquifer supplies Brady, Mason and Fredericksburg.5, 6, 9
