# Hickory Sands District AVA: geologic chronology, research notes

Compiled 2026-10-18 for the website's interactive timeline ("how the Hickory Sandstone Member, the district's sandy
soils and the Hickory Aquifer came to be"). Research notes, not site copy. Every statement below is tied to a source
listed in section F; where I computed something myself (the soil shares in 0.3) the method is given and it is labelled
as a derived figure, not a published one.

Conventions:

- Ages are in Ma (millions of years ago). Period, epoch and stage boundaries are from the ICS International
  Chronostratigraphic Chart **v2026/06** (Cohen, Harper, Gibbard & Car 2025, updated). "~" means the chart gives an
  approximate boundary.
- Confidence: **high** = stated in a primary or agency source I read; **medium** = stated in a reliable secondary
  summary, or an inference joining two sources I read; **low** = plausible but thinly sourced, or sources disagree.
- Unit names: the rock unit is **the Hickory Sandstone Member of the Riley Formation** (Cambrian). Older sources call it
  "Hickory sandstone", "Hickory Formation" or "Hickory Sandstone"; I keep their wording only when citing them. "Hickory
  Sands District" is only the AVA's name. Use "the Hickory Sandstone Member" in any site copy.
- Sources marked "abstract only" were read as published abstracts (paywalled full text). Sources marked "via" were read
  as cited by another source.

Key ICS v2026/06 boundaries used (Ma): Mesoproterozoic 1600-1000 (Ectasian 1400-1200, Stenian 1200-1000);
Cryogenian ~720-~635; Cambrian 538.8 +/- 0.6 to 486.85 +/- 1.5; Miaolingian ~506.5-~497.0 (Wuliuan ~506.5,
Drumian ~504.5, Guzhangian ~500.5); Furongian ~497.0-486.85 (Paibian ~497.0, Jiangshanian ~494.2, Stage 10 ~491.0);
Pennsylvanian 323.4 +/- 0.4 to 298.9 +/- 0.15 (Bashkirian 323.4, Moscovian 315.2, Kasimovian 307.0); Triassic base
251.902; Cretaceous base 143.1 +/- 0.6 (Aptian 121.4, Albian 113.2, Late Cretaceous 100.5); Paleogene base 66.00;
Oligocene base 33.9; Miocene 23.04-5.333.

## 0. Where the sand came from, and what the district's sand is today (the three questions)

Three questions: where the Hickory Sandstone Member was laid down, where its sand grains came from, and what the
district's loose sand is made of today.

### 0.1 Depositional setting of the Hickory Sandstone Member: marine, shoreline/tidal, or river?

**Best-supported answer (confidence: high for "shallow marine and shoreline, with river and wind-blown sand reworked
into it"; medium for the exact proportions, which no source quantifies):** the Hickory Sandstone Member is the basal
sand of the Cambrian Sauk transgression, the sea that spread across North America. Most of it was laid down, or last
reworked, in a shallow sea and along its shoreline: tidal channels and shoals, tidal flats, estuaries and beaches,
passing up into storm-influenced shelf sand under the Cap Mountain Limestone Member. Its lowest beds include sand and
gravel that rivers, alluvial fans and wind had spread over the weathered granite surface, which the rising sea then
reworked. It is not a deep-ocean deposit.

The change over time: early mapping only described the rock; Palmer's trilobites made it marine; Cornish (1975)
recast it as a tide-dominated estuarine and coastal deposit draped over buried hills; later work (Krause 1996; McBride
2002; Perez 2006; Kyle 2022) kept the marine and tidal reading for most of the unit but recognised a non-marine
component (alluvial-fan, braided-river and eolian sand) at the base and as a source that the sea reworked. The local
field descriptions (Nielson and Barker; Cullen) favour beach settings. I found no source that calls the Hickory a
deep-water deposit.

### 0.2 Provenance: local Llano granite, or distant sources?

**Best-supported answer (confidence: high that both local and distant sources contributed and that the western Llano
Uplift, where the district lies, is dominated by the distant signal in its zircons; medium for any statement about
how much of the quartz itself is local, because no source quantifies it):**

1. **Ultimately crystalline basement.** McBride et al. (2002) conclude the sand came ultimately from Precambrian
   basement rocks; Benson and Wilson (2015, USGS, citing Kyle and McBride) say the same. Originally it was
   feldspar-rich (subarkose to arkose); much of the feldspar was later dissolved during burial, so many beds are now
   "diagenetic" quartz arenites. Part of the Hickory's clean-quartz character is therefore made by diagenesis, not only
   by transport (McBride et al. 2002).
2. **A local, first-cycle component at the base.** Over and around the old granite hills the lowest Hickory is
   arkosic sand and conglomerate with granite pebbles and cobbles, resting on grus (Nielson and Barker 2013, Cherry
   Spring road cut; the Geologic Atlas of Texas description of the lower Hickory in the NW Llano area, carried in USGS
   SGMC: poorly sorted, rounded to subrounded, light-coloured sandstone with a granitic pebble conglomerate locally up
   to 75 ft thick). This is sand and gravel from the local granite, moved a short distance.
3. **A large, distant component.** The one systematic provenance study, Kyle et al. (2022), dated more than 1,700
   detrital zircons from the Hickory around the uplift:
   - 50% are 1550-1300 Ma (attributed to the Granite-Rhyolite Province basement north and north-west of the Grenville
     front, i.e. outside the Llano region) and 46% are 1300-1000 Ma (attributed to the local Grenville-age Llano
     basement); minor 1800-1600 Ma grains (Yavapai-Mazatzal Province, farther north-west) and Early Cambrian grains
     (Wichita Igneous Province, Oklahoma) also occur.
   - **Samples from the western Llano Uplift are dominated by the regional (distant) Granite-Rhyolite ages; samples
     from the eastern uplift are dominated by the local Grenville signature.** The district (Mason and McCulloch
     counties) is on the western and north-western flank.
   - The upper Hickory is more than 60% 1550-1300 Ma grains, with up to 10% older than 1600 Ma, suggesting the river
     headwaters reached farther into the continent with time.
   - The authors read this as both local sourcing and a regional drainage system delivering sand to the coast.
   TWDB (2016) likewise summarises older work as placing the source area to the north or north-west.
4. **Mature, rounded, partly recycled grains.** The upper Hickory has exceptionally well-rounded grains (Geologic Atlas
   description via SGMC); Kyle and McBride (via Benson and Wilson 2015) describe sand reworked from older river and dune
   deposits into well-sorted, well-rounded quartz arenites. Wind-rounding and repeated reworking before land plants is
   the usual explanation offered (Perez 2006; Nielson and Barker 2013).
5. **Comparable Cambrian sandstones in Texas and Oklahoma.** In west Texas, the Van Horn, Hazel and Lanoria formations
   and the basal Sauk sandstone carry zircons from the Grenville (Llano and equivalents), ~1.4 Ga Granite-Rhyolite and
   1.7-1.6 Ga Yavapai/Mazatzal provinces (Spencer et al. 2014, abstract read). Northward, Paige (1912, via USGS
   Geolex) correlated the Hickory with the Reagan Sandstone of Oklahoma, and Benson and Wilson (2015, citing Branson
   1944) note that the basal Lamotte Sandstone of Missouri may extend southward as the Reagan and the Hickory: one
   basal Sauk sand sheet laid on the basement as the sea advanced. I found no detrital-zircon study of the Reagan to
   compare provenance directly.

**What fraction is first-cycle local granite sand?** No source gives a number for the quartz. The zircon split
(50/46%) is a population count of one heavy mineral, not a share of the sand: zircon abundance differs between
source rocks, and the "distant" Granite-Rhyolite sources are themselves granites and rhyolites. So the defensible
statements are: (a) the basal beds near granite hills are largely local granite debris; (b) across the western uplift
the zircon record is dominated by sources hundreds of kilometres away; (c) the upper Hickory, the cleaner and
better-rounded part, carries the strongest distant signal.

### 0.3 The district's sandy soils and loose sand today: weathered Hickory sandstone or granite grus?

**Best-supported answer (confidence: medium-high; derived from NRCS data, see method):** inside the proposed boundary,
soils formed from weathered Cambrian sandstone, mostly the Hickory Sandstone Member, cover roughly five times the area
of soils formed from granite and granite grus. The district's sand at the surface is therefore mainly the Cambrian
sea's sand released again by weathering of the Hickory outcrop, with a smaller share of coarse feldspar-and-quartz
grus from the Precambrian granites, plus mixed river alluvium.

Derived shares of the area inside the proposed boundary (NRCS SSURGO via Soil Data Access, component-weighted; my
computation, 2026-10-18):

| Parent material of the soil series (from NRCS Official Series Descriptions) | Share of area |
|---|---|
| Cambrian sandstone, mostly the Hickory Sandstone Member: Hye 12.3%, Nebgen 10.1%, Acove 8.3%, Loneoak 7.8%, Campair 3.8%, Pontotoc 2.5%, Oben 1.6% | **46.5%** |
| Limestone (largely Cambrian limestone, e.g. Yates 6.3%; also Tarrant, Eckert and others) | 14.7% |
| Precambrian gneiss and schist (Katemcy, Ligon, Castell, Honeycreek) | 9.5% |
| Precambrian granite and granite grus (Keese 5.8%, which is granite and/or gneiss; Voca 1.2%; Lou 1.0%; Click 0.5%) | **8.5%** |
| Rock outcrop (granite 2.3%, sandstone 1.9%, limestone 0.9%, mixed 0.7%) | 5.8% |
| Mixed river alluvium (Fieldcreek and others) | 5.9% |
| Other or unnamed minor components, pits, water; other sandstone soils not tied to the Hickory | 9.2% |

For contrast, across all of Mason County (major components) the Cambrian-sandstone series and the granite/grus series
are about equal (15.0% vs 14.5%), so the boundary concentrates the Hickory-derived soils. McCulloch County as a whole
is dominated by limestone soils (Tarrant etc.); its Hickory soils are confined to its south-east corner, as Mason (1961)
describes the outcrop.

What the series descriptions say (NRCS OSDs, read 2026-10-18):

- Formed on the Hickory Sandstone Member or Cambrian sandstone: **Pontotoc** (slope alluvium and residuum from the
  Hickory sandstone; type location just north of Mason; established 1913), **Campair** (Cambrian sandstone, "primarily
  of the Hickory Formation"; type location on the Grit quadrangle west of Mason; sand surface), **Nebgen** (mostly the
  Hickory, some Lion Mountain and Cap Mountain), **Hye** (red Cambrian sandstone), **Loneoak** (Cambrian sandstone; type
  location near Loyal Valley, Mason County), **Acove** (Cambrian sandstone; type location near Voca, McCulloch County;
  loamy-sand surface with rounded quartz gravel), **Oben** (red glauconitic sandstone such as the Hickory).
- Formed on granite or grus: **Keese** (granite, granite grus or gneiss; coarse sandy loam with granite gravel),
  **Voca** (slope alluvium and residuum from grus and granite; granite, feldspar and quartz gravel; type location south
  of Voca), **Lou** and **Click** (granite grus).
- Note that several of these soils formed partly in **slope alluvium**: weathered sand (Hickory or grus) creeping and
  washing down onto footslopes. That modern downslope movement is real but local, metres to a few kilometres.

Method and limits: polygons of all SSURGO map units intersecting the proposed boundary
(`target/work/gis/hickory-sands-district-boundary-proposed-2026-10-15-area-EPSG4269.geojson`, simplified to 0.0004
deg) were intersected server-side in Soil Data Access (survey areas TX319 Mason 66.6%, TX307 McCulloch 19.3%, TX411
San Saba 12.5%, TX299 Llano 1.5%; SSURGO data as of 2026-09-03); each map unit's area was split by component percent
and summed by series. Coverage check: the intersected map-unit areas sum to the boundary polygon's own area
(0.0473496 deg2 each, i.e. complete coverage of the ~193.8 sq mi). Component percentages are NRCS estimates; "Cambrian sandstone" in the Hye, Loneoak and Acove
OSDs is not explicitly the Hickory (it could include the Lion Mountain or Welge sandstones). Treat the shares as
+/- a few percentage points.

lies within the Drumian-Guzhangian (Miaolingian), between ~504.5 and ~497 Ma on the ICS chart. The basal sands have no
fossils and the transgression was diachronous, so the base could be somewhat older in places; no radiometric date
exists for the unit.

## C. Claims to avoid

1. **"Deep ocean" or "ocean floor".** Say "a shallow Cambrian sea and its shoreline" (tidal flats, estuaries,
   beaches, shallow shelf).
2. **"The granite uplift broke up the sand" / "the sand washed off the uplift".** The granite is ~600 Myr older than
   the sand; the ring outcrop is erosional (chronology, section A).
3. **"Upper (Late) Cambrian" or "Dresbachian" as a modern age.** Those labels are older North American usage. On the
   current ICS chart the Hickory Sandstone Member is Miaolingian (Drumian-Guzhangian), ~504.5-497 Ma; say "about 500
   million years old". Nielson and Barker's "no older than about 540 million years" is an upper bound, not the age.
   SGMC and USGS Geolex labels ("Middle Cambrian", "Late Cambrian") also differ for this reason.
4. **"Half (or 46%) of the sand came from X".** The Kyle et al. percentages count dated zircon grains, not sand
   volume.
5. **"The Hickory is full of Grenville zircons from the north" (GSA Today 2025 wording).** The primary paper (Kyle et
   al. 2022) attributes the Grenville-age (1300-1000 Ma) grains to the local Llano basement and the northern supply to
   the older 1550-1300 Ma Granite-Rhyolite Province. Cite the primary paper.
6. **"Not from granite" (unqualified).** The distant sources are granitic and rhyolitic basement too; say "not from
   the local Llano granites" when that is meant.
7. **"Pure quartz from the start".** The sand was feldspar-rich; burial removed much of the feldspar (McBride et al.
   2002).
8. **"The Llano Uplift is high ground."** It is a structural high but a topographic basin: a lowland of old rock
   ringed by a rim of younger, harder rock (TWDB 2016 citing Wermund 1996).
9. **"Granite of the surrounding Llano Uplift"** (current wording on `docs/land.html`). The granite is the uplift's
   core, east and south-east of most of the district, not around it. And by the SSURGO computation about 10% of the
   area inside the proposed boundary is granite or gneiss soils or granite outcrop (8.5% + 2.3%), so "the line leaves
   the granite outside" overstates it; "leaves most of the granite core outside" is safer.
10. **"A Miocene Balcones uplift raised the Llano dome."** The Balcones Fault Zone is a belt of mostly down-to-the-
    south-east normal faults south-east of the uplift (late Oligocene-early Miocene per USGS WRI 94-4039). The dome
    is Pennsylvanian; post-Cretaceous uplift and erosion exposed it. TWDB 2016 itself labels the Balcones Fault Zone
    "Mesozoic" in one paragraph and Cenozoic in another; prefer the USGS dating.
11. **"The radioactivity comes from the granite" (alone).** Sources attribute it to uranium- and thorium-bearing
    minerals derived from the Precambrian rocks, including feldspars and minerals within the Hickory sandstone itself
    (TWDB page; TWDB 2016 citing Kreitler et al. 2013 and Preston 1996). Also note: the BEG Nicot et al. (2012)
    executive summary gives the radium limit as 15 pCi/L; the body correctly uses 5 pCi/L for combined radium and 15
    pCi/L for gross alpha. Exceedance rates vary with the dataset (40 of 458 Hickory samples for radium in TWDB 2016;
    about 65% of 95 Hickory wells in the Brown County study region in Nicot et al. 2012). Avoid a single percentage.
12. **Exact Brady dates from news reports.** Brady's radium violations and its TWDB-funded treatment plant
    (US$31.4 million) are reported by Inside Climate News / Texas Public Radio (Aug. 2026) and the Texas Observer; the
    compliance month is "this April" without a year. Verify with TCEQ or the city before using.
13. **"Voca" confusion.** The Voca *soil series* is a granite-grus soil; the Voca *frac sand* is mined from the Hickory
    Sandstone Member. Don't conflate them.
14. **Glauconite in the Hickory.** Sources differ (Palmer 1954: Hickory non-glauconitic, glauconite abundant in the Cap
    Mountain and Lion Mountain; McBride et al. 2002: glauconite formed at or near deposition; the Oben OSD mentions
    glauconitic Hickory sandstone). Don't feature it.
15. **"Hickory Formation"** (soil-survey shorthand) and bare "Hickory Sandstone". Use "the Hickory Sandstone Member (of
    the Riley Formation)".

## E. Open questions

1. **Kyle et al. 2022 full text.** Which of its samples lie in or near the district (western uplift, Mason and
   McCulloch counties)? Do the western samples include the lower Hickory, or mainly the upper? Does the paper contain
   the (U-Th)/He exhumation data (>6 km at 800-750 Ma) that Abbott and Cook 2025 cite to it? The abstract does not
   mention them. The paper (GSA Bulletin 135:1163-1177, doi:10.1130/B36389.1) is paywalled.
2. **Zircons younger than the fossils allow.** The Kyle et al. abstract reports Hickory zircons as young as ~487-485
   Ma, and says the younger Cambrian grains point to western sources. That is younger than the ~497 Ma upper limit
   from the trilobite zones (row 7). Possible explanations (lead loss in the grains, or samples from strata younger
   than the Hickory) are not discussed in the abstract; the paper would need to be read. Until then, keep the
   biostratigraphic age and don't quote the youngest zircon as a depositional age.
3. **Quartz grain type.** Cullen 2014 reports 99% monocrystalline quartz near Pontotoc; Benson and Wilson 2015
   (citing Levson et al. 2012) call the Brady sand polycrystalline. Possibly different intervals; unresolved.
4. **How much of the quartz is local?** No point-count or quartz-provenance fraction found. McBride et al. 2002 (full
   petrography) and Black 1988 (Hickory aquifer, Mason and McCulloch counties) may have it.
5. **Krause 1996 and Barnes and Bell 1977** were known only through abstracts and TWDB's summary. For any site copy
   about facies or "sequence stratigraphy", read them. I found no published sequence-stratigraphic study of the Hickory
   beyond Krause's thesis title.
6. **Precambrian ages** came from secondary summaries (Mosher's UT pages are offline; GSA Today 2025; GSA abstracts).
   Before printing single-pluton ages, check Walker 1992, Reese et al. 2000, Mosher et al. 2008 and Barker and Reed
   2010 directly, and identify the plutons nearest the district.
7. **Exhumation timing.** No thermochronology for the Cenozoic exhumation of the Llano area was found; the timeline
   should show post-Cretaceous erosion as a long, undated interval.
8. **SSURGO shares** are estimates (component percents, simplified boundary). If the site quotes them, rerun with the
   final boundary and say "about".
9. **Unit wording.** The site currently says "Hickory Sandstone Member formation". Formally it is "the Hickory
   Sandstone Member of the Riley Formation" (a member is a subdivision of a formation, so "Member formation" combines
   two ranks). Flagged only.
10. **Brady radium compliance** year and treatment method: confirm with TCEQ or the City of Brady.
11. **Current frac-sand operators** near Voca and Brady: the sources are 2014-2016 snapshots.
